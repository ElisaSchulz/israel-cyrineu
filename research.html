Winter wheat in the Southern Great Plains is increasingly sown late, because the weather leaves growers no choice. My work asks what that costs, physiologically, and what — if anything — genotype and management can do about it.

Does late-planted wheat really need a heavier seeding rate?

Standard guidance tells growers to raise the seeding rate when planting is delayed, compensating for reduced tillering and slower canopy closure. We tested that recommendation across two Oklahoma locations and three growing seasons, using eight full-season winter wheat genotypes and one short-season genotype (Butler's Gold), sown at a standard date in late October and a delayed date in early December, at recommended and elevated seeding rates.

Three results ran against expectation. Delayed sowing produced no consistent yield loss relative to standard sowing. The short-season genotype did not outperform full-season genotypes under delay. And raising the seeding rate brought no yield benefit at all — meaning growers forced to plant late can skip the added seed cost.

With Gustavo A. Slafer (University of Lleida), Brett F. Carver, and Amanda de Oliveira Silva

The physiology behind the delayed-sowing yield penalty

The core of my dissertation. Yield responses to planting date vary widely across genotypes, environments and management, and averaging over that variation hides the mechanism. I decompose the response into its physiological components — the duration of developmental phases, biomass accumulation and partitioning, and the traits that set grain number and grain weight — to identify which of them actually drive the penalty when it appears.

The practical goal is a set of traits that breeders and variety-testing programs can screen for, so that growers facing a delayed planting window have varieties genuinely suited to it rather than varieties merely tolerated in it.

Variety evaluation and Extension

With the OSU Small Grains Extension and Research program, I contribute to the annual trials that Oklahoma producers use to choose varieties: small grains variety performance tests, fall forage production and first hollow stem dates for dual-purpose wheat, and grain protein concentration across varieties and seasons.

This is the part of the work that reaches growers in the same year it is measured, and it keeps the dissertation questions anchored to decisions people are actually making.
